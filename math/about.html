A finite journey through infinite ideas.

Kosmos teaches mathematics and physics through the people who worked them out: a scribe in Babylon, a librarian in Alexandria measuring the Earth with a shadow, a scholar in Baghdad writing algebra for inheritance cases, a mathematician at NASA checking an orbit by hand.

WhyMathematics is a human story

Mathematics is usually taught as finished results with names attached: Pythagoras' theorem, Euclid's algorithm, Newton's laws. But every one of those was once a person, somewhere real, stuck on a real problem. How do you share seven loaves fairly among ten workers? How big is the world? Where will this planet be next month?

Kosmos puts you in that moment. Each lesson opens in a time and place, gives you a role in the scene, and lets you try the problem before showing the insight that solved it. You work through it with the original numbers, see why it works, and then see where the same idea is used today, in engineering, in navigation, in your phone. Lesson by lesson you move through four thousand years of history, across Babylon, Egypt, Greece, China, India, the Islamic world, Europe and the modern world, and the mathematics and science build on each other as they did in history.

The aim is simple: that you finish a lesson knowing not just how to do something, but who figured it out, why they needed it, and what it still does for us.

HomageWith thanks to Cosmos

The name and the spirit come from Cosmos. Carl Sagan's Cosmos: A Personal Voyage (1980), written by Sagan, Ann Druyan and Steven Soter, made the history of science feel like an adventure that belongs to everyone. Cosmos: A Spacetime Odyssey (2014), created by Ann Druyan and hosted by Neil deGrasse Tyson, carried that voyage to a new generation, followed by Cosmos: Possible Worlds. The idea of travelling through time to meet the people behind the ideas, and to feel the wonder they felt, is theirs. Kosmos is the ancient Greek word for an ordered world, and the root of "cosmos".

OpenBuilt on open knowledge, and open itself

History is only worth teaching if it's true, so everything in Kosmos can be checked.

  • Every lesson lists its sources, shown at the end of the lesson: the original publication, plus Wikipedia and the MacTutor History of Mathematics archive.
  • Quotations come from public-domain translations, checked word for word against scans at the Internet Archive: for example Rosen's 1831 translation of al-Khwarizmi and Heath's histories of Greek mathematics (1913, 1921).
  • Legends are labelled. Stories told centuries after the fact (the Eureka bath, the Leaning Tower drop, Newton's apple) appear in "Legend or history?" notes that say who first told them.
  • Pictures are reconstructions and say so. Characters are pixel figures, never claimed as likenesses. Period details, such as the Green Dome over Baghdad, the round kuphar boats on the Tigris and the three tiers of Alexandria's lighthouse, are sourced in the captions.
  • Maps are drawn from Natural Earth (public domain), with "see it today" links to OpenStreetMap (© OpenStreetMap contributors).
  • Open tools: maths is typeset with KaTeX (MIT licence); the fonts are Space Grotesk and Silkscreen (SIL Open Font Licence). The practice charts use Highcharts, which is free for non-commercial use but not open source.
  • The app itself is open source under the MIT licence: plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no build step, no accounts and no tracking. Your progress stays in your own browser.
Found a mistake, a better source, or a story that deserves a lesson? Corrections are welcome: open an issue or a pull request on GitHub.

Made togetherA human and an AI, building in the open

Kosmos is a collaboration between a person and an AI model, and it's worth being plain about who did what.

Idea, direction, judgement
Raj Maitra
  • Had the idea: teach maths as the history of the people who made it.
  • Set the direction and made the calls: adding physics, the DOS-era pixel-art style, maps of where it happened, the calculator, the name Kosmos.
  • Tries every lesson and asks for changes, from reading comfort to which diagrams a problem needs.
  • Reviews and publishes the work.
Research, writing, code
Claude (an AI model by Anthropic, working in Claude Code)
  • Researched the history and checked it against primary sources and scans, fixing its own misquotations when the originals disagreed.
  • Wrote the lessons, problems, hints and worked solutions.
  • Designed and wrote the software: the lesson player, the pixel-art scene and character engine, the maps, the interactive diagrams, the calculator, and the automated checks.
  • Wrote the documentation, including this page.

The way of working is a conversation. Raj proposes a direction; Claude researches, drafts and builds, and runs its own checks: every answer through a validator, every lesson played end to end in a real browser, and every picture reviewed from screenshots. Raj then tries it and asks for changes. AI can be confidently wrong, which is exactly why every fact here comes with a source you can follow.

To run it yourself, serve the repository folder with any static web server, because the lessons are loaded as data files:

git clone https://github.com/rmaitra/rmaitra.github.io.git
cd rmaitra.github.io
python3 -m http.server 8000      # then open http://localhost:8000/math/
node math/tools/check-stories.mjs  # validate the lessons after editing

A new lesson is mostly data: add its steps, characters and pictures to stories.json and the timeline picks it up. The design notes explain every step type, picture part and rule for writing a lesson.
